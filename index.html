Searching for X17 at n_TOF (CERN)

2025 – present · Affiliated Researcher, The University of Manchester

A series of anomalies in nuclear transitions — first reported by the ATOMKI group — show an excess of e⁺e⁻ pairs at large opening angles, consistent with the decay of a hypothetical ~17 MeV boson, "X17". We are mounting an independent search at CERN's n_TOF facility in the ³He(n, e⁺e⁻)⁴He channel, using a pressurized ³He target surrounded by four micro-TPC Micromegas tracking walls and layered scintillator calorimetry.

I am technical lead for the DREAM-based data acquisition — the software front end and the full acquisition chain — and the on-site contact person for the experiment's 40-day data-taking run, covering commissioning, flammable-gas operations, and the scintillator trigger logic across six CAEN N1081B programmable logic units. The Geant4 studies that established the feasibility of a thermal-neutron measurement drove a redesign of the calorimetry, and the operations stack I built — fifteen standing monitors with automated alerting, a live shift dashboard, automated EOS transfer — is what lets the experiment run unattended.

Why a bump is suspicious: pairs from ordinary internal pair creation (IPC) fall off steeply with opening angle. A new particle of mass m decaying in flight piles up near its kinematic angle θ ≈ 2·asin(mc²/E) — a peak exactly where the background is quietest.

Micromegas & micro-pattern gaseous detectors

CEA Paris-Saclay · micro-TPC construction · µRWELL in the SPS H4 beamline · CyMBaL for ePIC

Much of my work happens one centimeter above an anode plane. A Micromegas is a two-stage gaseous detector: a charged particle ionizes the gas in a few-millimeter drift gap, the electrons drift to a micromesh, and a thin ~100 µm gap below it at very high field multiplies each electron ~10⁴-fold. The charge induced on segmented anode strips gives the particle's position.

At Saclay I helped build the 40 × 40 × 3 cm micro-TPC Micromegas detectors used in the X17 search and characterized them on a cosmic-ray bench. In the SPS H4 beamline I measured a µRWELL detector's efficiency and charge-spreading response, and identified a non-amplifying region that no operating voltage could recover. For the future Electron-Ion Collider I test prototypes for CyMBaL, the barrel tracking layer of ePIC, and retrofitted a cosmic muon telescope with a silicon tracker for rapid prototype QA.

Fire a track and watch the measurement happen: ionize → drift → avalanche → cluster → centroid.

Luminosity & vernier scans at sPHENIX (RHIC)

2023 – 2025 · Postdoctoral Researcher, CEA Paris-Saclay (DPhN)

Every cross-section a collider experiment publishes is divided by a luminosity — so someone has to measure how hard the beams actually collide. In a vernier scan the two beams are deliberately swept across each other while recording the collision rate: the rate traces out the beam-overlap integral, and its width converts raw counts into absolute luminosity. I led the sPHENIX effort to measure luminosity in both Au+Au and p+p collisions, and wrote bunch-collider, a full beam–beam simulation with crossing angles and hourglass (β*) effects that constrains the beam parameters beyond the naive Gaussian fit.

Alongside that I built data-quality tools for the 2024 p+p spin physics program, deployed DAQ monitoring with a GUI that alerts shift crews to beam losses and abnormal rates, and helped re-commission the Zero Degree Calorimeters so beam polarization could be verified at the collision point.

Quark–gluon plasma & the STAR Beam Energy Scan

2016 – 2023 · Ph.D., UCLA · advisor Huan Huang

Microseconds after the Big Bang, the universe was a quark–gluon plasma — and colliding heavy nuclei recreates droplets of it. My doctoral work with the STAR collaboration searched the QCD phase diagram for the conjectured critical point using event-by-event proton multiplicity fluctuations across the Beam Energy Scan. I developed a novel observable using azimuthal partitions, sensitive to proton clustering and so to a first-order phase transition — the method paper is Phys. Rev. C 110, 064905.

Below, collision geometry drives everything: the impact parameter sets the participant zone (orange), which sets how many particles come out. Run many events and watch the proton distribution build — the analysis lives in how its width compares to the uncorrelated baseline.
