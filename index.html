Searching for X17 at n_TOF (CERN)

2025 – present · CEA Saclay group at the CERN n_TOF facility

A series of anomalies in nuclear transitions — first reported by the ATOMKI group — show an excess of e⁺e⁻ pairs at large opening angles, consistent with the decay of a hypothetical ~17 MeV boson, "X17". I work on an independent search at CERN's n_TOF facility: I develop the Micromegas-based detection, ran the GEANT4 sensitivity studies for the gas and geometry choices, and built and operate the experiment's DAQ and monitoring stack through our beam campaigns.

Why a bump is suspicious: pairs from ordinary internal pair creation (IPC) fall off steeply with opening angle. A new particle of mass m decaying in flight piles up near its kinematic angle θ ≈ 2·asin(mc²/E) — a peak exactly where the background is quietest.

Micromegas detectors & picosecond timing

CEA Saclay · detector R&D, cosmic-bench characterization, PICOSEC

Much of my work happens one centimeter above an anode plane. A Micromegas is a two-stage gaseous detector: a charged particle ionizes the gas in a few-millimeter drift gap, the electrons drift to a micromesh, and a thin ~100 µm gap below it at very high field multiplies each electron ~10⁴-fold. The charge induced on segmented anode strips gives the particle's position. At Saclay I characterize Micromegas and µRWELL detectors on a cosmic-ray tracking bench, and I work on PICOSEC, a Cherenkov-based Micromegas that pushes time resolution to tens of picoseconds.

Fire a track and watch the measurement happen: ionize → drift → avalanche → cluster → centroid.

Luminosity & vernier scans at sPHENIX (RHIC)

2024 – 2025 · run QA and absolute-luminosity analysis for the sPHENIX p+p run

Every cross-section a collider experiment publishes is divided by a luminosity — so someone has to measure how hard the beams actually collide. In a vernier scan the two beams are deliberately swept across each other while recording the collision rate: the rate traces out the beam-overlap integral, and its width converts raw counts into absolute luminosity. For sPHENIX I worked on the 2024 p+p run QA and vernier-scan analysis, and wrote bunch-collider, a C++/Python library simulating colliding bunches with crossing angles and hourglass (β*) effects to model the scan beyond the naive Gaussian.

Quark–gluon plasma & the STAR Beam Energy Scan

Ph.D. — UCLA · proton-multiplicity fluctuations in Au+Au collisions

Microseconds after the Big Bang, the universe was a quark–gluon plasma — and colliding heavy nuclei recreates droplets of it. My doctoral work with the STAR collaboration searched the QCD phase diagram for the conjectured critical point using event-by-event proton multiplicity fluctuations across the Beam Energy Scan: near a critical point, correlations should make the proton count fluctuate more than independent emission allows.

Below, collision geometry drives everything: the impact parameter sets the participant zone (orange), which sets how many particles come out. Run many events and watch the proton distribution build — the analysis lives in how its width compares to the uncorrelated baseline.
