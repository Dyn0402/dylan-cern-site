Roughly 324 hours at a keyboard

Time is estimated by gap-based sessionization: prompts and commits are anchors that prove someone was present and acting, consecutive anchors closer together than a threshold form one work block, and the blocks are summed. At a 20-minute threshold that gives 324 hours across 41 days — a mean of 7.9 h and a median of 7.4 h per day.

The threshold matters, so here is the whole curve rather than one number: 248 h at 10 min, 291 h at 15, 324 h at 20, 373 h at 30, 420 h at 45. Everything below uses 20 minutes.

Four phases, and one crossover

The campaign divides cleanly. Commissioning split roughly half and half. Once beam arrived, operations took nearly two thirds and held it for sixteen days — the stretch containing the comb, the veto, the 10 GbE upgrade and the trigger rebuilds. Then, from 24 July, analysis pulled ahead. When beam ended on 6 August, simulation took a third of everything.

On the measure

Two measures appear here and neither is hours-at-work. Prompt counts weight a five-second instruction the same as one that launches a twelve-hour scan. Estimated time fixes part of that by measuring spans rather than counts, but it can only see intervals bracketed by anchors — so a long unattended scan you watched from the control room registers as idle, and its run ticks appear on the timeline with no bar above them.

Both measures are blind in the same direction: cabling, accesses, shifts, meetings and bench work leave no anchor at all, so operations is under-counted, not over. The real hours were higher than 324 and the real DAQ share higher than 53.7%. Commits were computed as an independent cross-check and agree on the shape (43% operations, 26% analysis, 31% simulation). Classification is by repository and working directory, so a prompt issued from the analysis repo about a DAQ problem counts as analysis. Non-X17 work on these machines is excluded entirely.
