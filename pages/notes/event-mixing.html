What event mixing is doing in this analysis

Event mixing builds a fake dataset out of real tracks that never shared a trigger. It is the analysis's measurement of what an accidental two-track event looks like — and right now it is also the single most uncomfortable number on the page, because the observed opening-angle spectrum is indistinguishable from it.

Campaign pass · 62,729 real pairs from 33 runs, each answered by one mixed pair · built 2026-09-11

What it does. For every real pair of tracks found in one trigger, it draws a replacement pair — same two chambers, same track selection, same run — from two different triggers.

What it models. The accidental component: two unrelated particles that happened to land in the same readout window. Everything about the apparatus survives the shuffle; only the physical link between the two tracks is removed.

What it is telling us. Across the campaign the observed opening-angle spectrum matches this null better than it matches any pair spectrum — IPC or X17 — by a factor of 4–21 in χ². The inclusive sample is consistent with being all accidentals. Only after a hard timing cut does the data begin to move off the null, and then it moves in the wrong direction.

1The estimator, exactly

Mixing is a permutation, and the details of which permutation decide whether the answer means anything.

The input is the stage-3 track table: gated, angle-calibrated tracks that point within 30 mm of the beam axis. A real pair is any two of those tracks inside one trigger. For each real pair, in source_imaging._pairs_mixed:

  1. read off its chamber combination, e.g. A–C;
  2. draw one track from the A pool and one from the C pool, within the same run;
  3. reject and redraw if the two draws come from the same trigger;
  4. emit it as one mixed pair, and compute exactly the same observables — opening angle, closest approach, vertex position.

Two choices in there are not cosmetic and were both got wrong on a first pass. The pools are drawn only from tracks that actually form real pairs, not from every track in the run: a trigger that produced two tracks is a busier trigger and its tracks are not drawn from the same distribution as a lone one. Mixing against the full track pool made the null look better than the data and the lift came out below 1. And the mixed frame carries both event keys, key1 and key2; when it carried only the first, a downstream timing study silently read the second track's scintillator out of the wrong event.

2What it is meant to model

One component of the observed sample, isolated by removing the one thing mixing destroys.

Every two-track trigger is one of two things. Either a single interaction produced both legs — an internal pair, an X17 decay, a scatter — or two unrelated particles arrived close enough in time to be read out together. The analysis wants the first; the trigger hands it both.

Mixing is an attempt to measure the second box directly, using the data itself rather than a simulation. The logic is that a mixed pair is guaranteed accidental: the two tracks physically could not have come from one interaction, because they arrived minutes apart. Everything else about them is real — real detector, real acceptance, real efficiency, real occupancy, real beam conditions.

Preserved by the shuffle

  • each chamber's single-track rate and angular distribution
  • the geometric acceptance, including the 90° chamber layout that decides most of the opening angle
  • per-run conditions — gas, gain, threshold, dead channels (mixing never crosses a run)
  • the track quality cuts and the pairing rule
  • the topology composition, pair for pair

Destroyed by the shuffle

  • any correlation between the two tracks
  • the common vertex — two tracks from different triggers cannot point at the same place except by chance
  • the common interaction time
  • and, unavoidably, anything measured relative to the event's own trigger — see §5

So the comparison is a difference of correlations. If the data contains real pairs, the data must differ from the mixed sample in some variable that a common origin controls: opening angle, vertex position, or closest approach. If it does not differ, the data contains no pairs that the variable can see.

3What it says about the data

This is the headline, and it is a negative one.

Put the mixed shape into the same χ² table as the physics models, on the same footing — every shape normalised to the observed total, no free parameter anywhere — and it wins everywhere, by a wide margin:

The honest reading

This is not evidence against X17. It is evidence that the inclusive two-track sample is dominated by accidental coincidences, at a level where a pair signal of any plausible size is invisible underneath it. The measurement that has to come first is not a spectrum — it is a selection that removes accidentals.

4Where mixing earns its keep

The same null, applied to a cleaner sample and to a different variable, does start to separate.

Timing-selected pairs

The accidental-timing study established that in a two-arm event one arm is the trigger and the other fires at essentially a random time — median |Δt| of 171 ns against a 5 ns single-arm reference. The production accept window of (−100, +60) ns is wide enough that the plastic scintillators' own accidental rate lands something inside it almost every time. Tighten it to the peak core — |t| ≤ 30 ns per arm and |t1 − t2| ≤ 20 ns between them — and the sample drops to a few hundred pairs that are genuinely prompt with each other.

But read the direction before celebrating

The opposing deviation is not a bump in the X17 window. The per-bin pulls run 128°: -3.1σ, 142°: -5.8σ, 158°: +2.4σ, 172°: +5.1σ — a deficit through 120–150° and an excess piled against 180°. That is the signature of one particle crossing two opposing chambers, which registers as a perfectly time-coincident “pair” at ~180°. Pairs above 170° are already cut, and the survivors still pile up against that edge: 20.1 % of the tight opposing sample sits in the last 5° before the cut, against 11.8 % of the loose sample.

So the tight cut works — it removes accidentals — but it enriches a correlated background that event mixing cannot model at all, because that background really is one correlated object. Mixing separates pairs from accidentals. It cannot separate pairs from other correlated things.

The vertex, where mixing does deliver a signal

The opening angle is a weak discriminator here because the geometry dominates it. The vertex is a much stronger one: two tracks from different triggers cannot converge on the beam axis except by accident.

5Where mixing is not a valid null at all

Two failures on record, both of which produced a physics-looking number before they were caught.

Trigger-referenced timing

Every hit time in this dataset is measured relative to its own event's trigger. There is no common clock across events. So a mixed Δt = t1 − t2 is a difference of two numbers that each sit near zero for their own, unrelated reasons — and the answer you get depends entirely on which hits you put in the pool.

Symmetric mixing on a triggered sample

The first version of this idea, at stage 1, permuted every chamber's contents independently across triggers. It reported a 1.45 % accidental two-arm rate against 1.03 % observed, and the 29 % “deficit” was briefly read as a physics statement.

It was the trigger. The DAQ fires on a wall-plus-plastic coincidence in any one arm, so every recorded event already contains one guaranteed particle, and the four arms are anti-correlated by construction — whichever arm the trigger particle went into, it did not go into the other three. Mixing all four destroys that constraint and predicts the second track at p², which it is not. event_mixing_background now raises NotImplementedError rather than returning a number.

The repaired version holds the trigger arm fixed and mixes only the others. It is correct, and it is also vacuous: permuting a flag among events conserves that flag's total exactly, so the mixed and observed second-arm probabilities can differ only through clumping. Measured: 6.2335 % observed against 6.3154 ± 0.0082 % mixed. No permutation of counts can separate a pair partner from an unrelated track — what distinguishes them is timing and geometry, which is why mixing belongs downstream of the waveform reconstruction and not at stage 1.

6What this does not rule out

  • A pair signal below the accidental floor. The inclusive spectrum matching the null means accidentals dominate, not that the pair rate is zero. No limit is quoted from this comparison, because the mixed sample carries no normalisation.
  • A signal visible in a variable not yet used. The opening angle is heavily constrained by the 90° chamber geometry, which mixing preserves exactly — making it the least sensitive observable available. The vertex already behaves differently (§4), and the intra-chamber slope-selected sample is a real excess over this null.
  • The opposing tight-pair deviation being partly real. It is dominated by collinear punch-through, but the 120–150° deficit is not obviously explained by that alone, and the sample is only 571 pairs.
  • Anything about the accidental rate. The Poisson estimate Ntrig × pi × pj over-predicts the observed pair count by 2–4× in every chamber combination, because the trigger correlates the arms. Normalising the accidental component remains the leading systematic and is stated as such rather than guessed.
