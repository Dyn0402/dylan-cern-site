Which ion drifts? Charge transfer vs Penning

Ar/iC₄H₁₀ 95/5 · MX17 Micromegas · 2026-10-15

Two different processes both end with an isobutane ion. They are easy to mix up because both happen for the same reason: argon's energy levels sit above isobutane's ionization energy. They change different parts of the signal.

Ion charge transfer

Ar⁺ + iC₄H₁₀ → Ar + iC₄H₁₀⁺

An ion passes its charge to an isobutane molecule within about 1 ns. This decides which ion drifts, so it sets the ion mobility and the speed of the ion tail.

Penning transfer

Ar* + iC₄H₁₀ → Ar + iC₄H₁₀⁺ + e⁻

An excited neutral argon atom ionizes isobutane and frees an extra electron. This raises the gain and changes the α(E) slope (EnablePenningTransfer, rP).

2 · Charge transfer: which ion drifts

Ions drift left toward the mesh. Watch the speed trace underneath. Each Ar⁺–Ar collision moves the charge onto an argon atom at rest, so the Ar⁺ trace keeps dropping back to zero. After the hop onto isobutane, the hydrocarbon ion has no partner it can hand its charge to, so collisions only slow it down and its average speed is higher.

3 · Penning: an extra electron

An avalanche electron excites an argon atom instead of ionizing it. In pure argon that energy leaves as a UV photon. With isobutane present, a collision with the Ar* uses the energy to ionize an isobutane molecule, which adds one more free electron to the avalanche.

4 · Why it matters: the ion tail

Same avalanche, same 128 µm gap at 490 V, two mobilities. The ions start near the strips, because the avalanche grows exponentially toward the anode. They then drift up to the mesh, and the induced current lasts as long as the slowest ones are still moving. Both curves carry the same total charge; the hydrocarbon ion delivers it about 2.4 times sooner.
