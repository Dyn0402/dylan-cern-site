4 µA

Answer: no water is needed at LNL

The beam deposits only 1.1 W per µA. The diamond carries the heat sideways to the ring, so the film sits only ~15 K above the ring at 4 µA. Without water, the ring sits above the flange by P × R, where R is the thermal resistance of the holder. A solid Cu arm (Ø10 mm × 15 cm, 4.8 K/W) keeps the film at 24 °C at 0.5 µA and 56 °C at the full 4 µA. That is far below the 150 °C limit for Li, and the 20 µm film needs only ~0.12–0.5 µA anyway.

  • The holder must be a conductor. A thin stainless tube (350 K/W) would melt the Li at 1 µA. Use a Cu or Al arm or a thick Cu strap to a flange.
  • Leaving water out is better, not just allowed. A water leak into the chamber would attack the metallic Li (Li + H₂O) and wreck the ≤ 10⁻⁸ mbar water spec. Water lines also add mass near the leptons.
  • Electrically isolate the target but keep it thermally connected so it can serve as the current monitor. An AlN or sapphire washer in the arm adds ~0.1–1 K/W (ESTIMATE).
  • Water becomes worth it above ~10 µA (a Cu arm reaches 150 °C at ~13 µA; a water-cooled ring at ~30 µA). That is the regime of other machines, not LNL's AN2000 or CN.

Thin Li, then stop in the backing

Pierfrancesco asked whether the Li should be thick enough to stop the protons. The design does the opposite on purpose: the Li is just thick enough for the beam to cross the 1030 keV resonance, and the protons then stop in the Mo backing, where they make nothing. A proton slowing down in Li passes through every energy below where it starts. Below the 18.15 MeV resonance sits the narrow but strong 441 keV one (17.64 MeV γ). A Li film that stops the beam takes 77 % of its γ₀ from that line, 0.5 MeV from the signal line, which the calorimetry cannot separate. Stopping the beam in the Li does gain signal (×17.8 the thin Li₂O against ×8.2 at 20 µm, about ×2.2), but it brings 3.4 γ of the 17.64 MeV line for every signal γ, and it makes the 441 keV share depend on everything that shifts the beam energy. The middle ground, ~40 µm (×14, 3.3 %), is possible but leaves little margin before the cliff.

20 µm beam

How to choose the thickness

  • Thick enough that the beam leaves the Li below the resonance (≲ 950 keV at 1.10 MeV in): the γ₀ yield then grows almost linearly with thickness. 20 µm gives ×8.2 the thin Li₂O baseline.
  • Thin enough that the beam leaves well above 441 keV. The 441 share stays near 1 % up to ~30 µm, then hits a cliff at ~45–49 µm (1.10 MeV in), where the exit energy reaches 441 keV.
  • Room on both sides: 15–25 µm gives exit energies of 930–810 keV, so a ±25 % thickness error, carbon build-up, or a beam-energy drift of tens of keV changes nothing important (yield −7 % for −10 % Li).
  • The backing then has to stop 0.87 MeV protons cleanly. 8 µm of Mo does it (range ~5 µm) with no γ-rays, the charge conducted away and the H held in a metal. That is the job of the beam stop in this design.
  • A higher beam energy moves the window up. At 1.20 MeV, ~25–40 µm is the equivalent choice. LNL's 1.10 MeV with 20 µm keeps the whole resonance inside the film.

Does the beam need to be stopped?

The protons stop somewhere whether we want it or not. They leave the Li at 0.87 MeV and have only ~5 µm of range left in Mo (~6–8 µm in diamond, my estimate). Any backing thick enough to carry heat stops them. The only alternative is a transmission target: Li on a backing a few µm thick, with a beam dump downstream. It fails three ways:

  • Li is not self-supporting.
  • A few µm of anything cannot carry heat to a rim. 20 µm of Li alone conducts 1.7 mW/K sideways; 50 µm of diamond conducts ~0.1 W/K.
  • The target can no longer integrate the beam current, and that current is the normalisation.

So the design choice is the material the beam stops in. Stopping in the target is even useful: the target becomes a Faraday cup.

Copper instead of diamond?

Days to 3σ at R(ATOMKI) at 1 µA, relative comparison as in L9. Cu 100 µm: the fit (0.35) sits above the counting estimate (0.30) because of a low-statistics EPC template. Thermal: opt_thermal.substrate().

  • The trade is thickness. For the same heat conduction, Cu costs ~40× more radiation length than diamond. So Cu is cheap for the leptons only as a thin foil, and a thin foil spreads heat poorly.
  • A 25 µm Cu foil matches the diamond design for the leptons (0.21 vs 0.22 d/µA) and is cold at the 0.12–0.5 µA the 20 µm Li needs. Its headroom ends at ~5 µA, LNL's maximum.
  • Machinable Cu (≥ 100 µm) costs ×1.6–1.9 in days, the same as the 1 mm diamond: legs below 90° cross it edge-on (σ68 of those 14–20°), and external conversion grows to 17–31 % of the background.
  • Hydrogen: the beam stops in the Cu, which blisters from ~10¹⁹ /cm²: 19 d at 0.25 µA, 9 d at 0.5 µA, 1.2 d at 4 µA. One 3σ measurement takes ~1 d at 0.25 µA, so a foil lasts a run. Swap targets, or add 8 µm Mo on the Cu (not simulated).
  • What Cu does well: it conducts (charge, current readout); Li wets it with no intermetallics (Li on Cu is the standard BNCT target); no γ above 1 MeV; cheap; and no unmeasured blistering question.
Verdict. Use the machinability where it costs nothing: the ring, clamp and holder are Cu anyway. A thick Cu substrate under the leptons is the wrong choice for ⁸Be. A 25 µm Cu foil clamped in a Cu ring is a real alternative at LNL's operating point: same reach, simpler, no materials gap. It gives up the thermal headroom (5 vs 30 µA) and lasts ~1–3 weeks per target. Keep diamond for running at the full 4 µA, for long targets, or for other machines.

Pierfrancesco's reply to the LNL slides called the target the critical issue. He raised the points below (paraphrased) and proposed his own design. Each card gives the short answer first.

On 24 Oct Pierfrancesco proposed two target shapes. In both, the target is the vacuum wall at the end of the beam pipe, with forced air outside and no target chamber. Each is shown in 3D with X17 pairs from our Geant4 simulation, and what they would cross.

In short. The question is how much material the e⁺e⁻ cross on their way to the arms. Every gram of it blurs the opening angle and adds pairs from converted γ-rays. The dome works in Be or Al (+Mo), not in 200 µm Cu. The tilted diamond window works if the last ~40 mm of tube before it is thin Al or CFRP, not steel. Make it Ø20 mm, 200–300 µm diamond in thin Ti rings (26 Oct): it holds 1 bar and scatters as little as the design. Heat is easy for both at 4 µA.
