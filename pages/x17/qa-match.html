The match — DREAM ↔ n_TOF

420 segments · slim campaign of 12 August 2026 · in progress

Two independent DAQs recorded the same beam on two independent clocks, and neither is physics on its own: the Micromegas measure the pair, the n_TOF scintillators say when the neutron arrived. This page is the join between them — how each piece of beam was put on one clock, how well it worked, and how much of it is not joined yet.

  • 94.9 % matched median over the joined segments
  • 0.059 % accidental measured, not modelled
  • 6.7 ns residual flat from 1 ms to 80 ms
  • 144 Beam hours joined DREAM triggers with n_TOF hits attached
  • 236 Segments to go failed or not yet attempted

What a segment is, and why the clock is fitted per bunch

A segment is one DREAM sub-run crossed with the one n_TOF run that was taking beam at the same moment. It is the smallest thing that can carry its own clock fit, and the reason is physical: the DREAM timestamp clock wanders about a part per million from burst to burst, which is 60 ns of drift across an 80 ms bunch — several times the width of the accept window. A single offset and rate for a whole run cannot absorb that, so the fit carries a per-bunch offset and rate correction on top:

t_nTOF = t_DREAM · (1 + K + δkb) + T₀ + aarm + δab
accept  |t_candidate − t_nTOF| < 25 ns

K and T₀ are the clock rate ratio and offset, a_arm is a fixed offset per detector arm, and the two δ terms are fitted per bunch. None of these transfer. The same constants fitted on a different processing of the same run leave a 45 ns offset and a 1.35 % rate error, so every segment is fitted from its own data and nothing reads a stored constant.

The clock is fully fitted before anything is cut. No hit is ever discarded on a provisional calibration: the fit runs on a candidate list, and only then is every scintillator hit within ±1 µs of the corrected prediction written out — along with the same width taken 100 µs later, which is the control window the accidental rate is measured from.

Is the per-bunch fit honest?

It is fitted on matched triggers and then used to match, which is a real circularity and was audited rather than argued away. About a hundred matched triggers per bunch constrain two parameters; a split-half test gives ρ = +0.996, which is 0.92 ppm of real drift against 0.06 ppm of fit noise; and the in-sample to cross-validated gap is a few tenths of a percent, reported per segment in the table above.

The decisive test is different: efficiency in a ±500 ns window is identical to five decimal places with and without the per-bunch correction. The correction concentrates matches into the accept window; it does not create them.

What the QA checks, and what it cannot see

Each joined segment is judged against nineteen absolute checks — the fitted rate in a physical range, the coarse peak above its floor, efficiency, accidental rate, cross-validation gap, residual centring and width, arm offsets against the reference run, per-bunch scatter, whether flash triggers correctly carry no hits, and whether the coincidence is contained in the slim window rather than cut by it. On top of that sits a population test: a segment can pass every absolute check and still be the only one in the campaign whose T₀ sits 300 ns from its neighbours, and that is the failure mode that produced a silently mis-timed slim in the first place. Population outliers are reported and never silently upgraded into a failure — being unusual is a reason to look, not proof of being wrong.

All 170 joined segments pass all nineteen checks, and that number has to be read with the failures beside it. A mis-joined segment fails its clock fit and writes no file, so the QA layer never sees it. The QA layer is clean because the broken segments are absent. That is exactly why this page defaults to coverage rather than quality, and why the segments that produced nothing are rows here rather than a footnote.

The 107 that failed, and why the beam is not lost

The cause is known, it is in our own code, and it is not in the data. pulse_match’s offset scan compares counts only, which is degenerate under the accelerator supercycle: several offsets score identically and it silently kept the most negative one, shifting every bunch number by 20 to 41. A wrong bunch assignment leaves no coincidence at all, so the segment fails loudly and writes nothing.

Both bugs found in this campaign destroy data rather than corrupting it, which is the good failure mode: the 170 shipped products are sound and need no re-validation. One lost hour and one lost sliver were re-joined on the fixed code through an otherwise unmodified chain and came back at 95.47 % and 95.56 % efficiency — fleet-typical. Nothing has been re-slimmed in production yet.

And fourteen of the 170 accepted segments were decided by a margin of two clusters or fewer. They pass every check and were a coin flip away from the same failure. They should be verified by an explicit offset scan before use. This is stated here rather than in a private log because a page that showed 170 green rows and said nothing else would be actively misleading.

Provenance

Numbers on this page come from the per-segment clock_qa.json records the slim pipeline writes beside each output file, joined to the campaign inventory — which is what makes the failed and unattempted segments visible at all — by scripts/freeze_x17_match.py in this site’s repository. The calibration itself is documented in ntof_dream_merge/DREAM_NTOF_CALIBRATION.md, and the machine- readable constants for the reference pair live in nTof_x17_DAQ/calibrations/dream_ntof/. Two working notes cover the ground in more depth: the clock QA note, which is where the apparent ±150 ns window was resolved into per-bunch drift, and the join mislock note.
