The other four pages ask whether the data is there and intact. This one asks whether the reconstruction is sound, which needs a different shape of answer: a campaign median cannot tell a chamber that is uniformly mediocre from one that was fine until a Tuesday afternoon, and only the second is fixable. So this page drills down — 36 runs, then that run's file tags a few minutes apart, then that sub-run's individual tracks, then one track and every column it has.

The reconstruction is stable run to run; the angle scale is not.

The fit quality, the strip counts and the charge sit inside their own arm's campaign band for almost every run — the outlier table below is short, and what is in it is one variable on one arm, not a run that went bad. What does move is k, the angle scale, and it moves together on all three arms across one contiguous 48-hour block, runs 128–147. That is the open blocker as of 10 September 2026, and it is the reason every panel here that shows an angle also shows the scale it was computed with.

Levels one and two are quantiles frozen by tracking_qa. Level three is computed in your browser, from the same columns that script profiled — so the two can be checked against each other, and the histogram draws the frozen median as a tick for exactly that reason.

The drift gap check

The one place the reconstruction can be checked against a ruler: no track may drift further than the gap is deep. A frac over gap above zero means the applied k is too small for that arm, and k min for gap is what it would have to be. This is the angle-scale blocker, stated geometrically.

How this page works

Three files, one per level, because six orders of magnitude do not fit in one download.

The campaign and the runs come from data/x17-trackqa.json — about 170 kB, loaded with the page. A run's tags come from data/trackqa/<run>.json.gz, one file per run, fetched when you open it and inflated in the browser (CERN's Apache does not compress on the fly, so the gzip is done at freeze time and undone here). Both are frozen by scripts/freeze_x17_trackqa.py from the CSVs sept26_prelim_analysis.tracking_qa writes.

The tracks are the unusual part. They live in one parquet shard per sub-run, 10–20 MB each and 4.7 GB in total, and this page never downloads one. Parquet stores each column in its own contiguous byte range, and CERN's web hosting honours HTTP Range requests, so the reader — hyparquet, vendored into js/lib/, the only third-party code on this site — reads the footer, works out which ranges hold the columns being plotted, and asks for exactly those. Seven columns of a 121 000-track sub-run cost a few MB of a 19 MB file and about two seconds, almost all of it per-request latency rather than transfer; each further variable after that is a few hundred milliseconds, and changing a cut costs nothing at all, because the columns are already in memory. The decoding happens in a worker so the page does not freeze while it runs.

The shards are built by sept26_prelim_analysis.trackqa_shards and carry every column tracking_qa profiles, plus the geometry and the n_TOF columns already merged into the track table. Rejected tracks are kept — they are half the table, and shipping only the survivors would make the one question the gate raises, what does it throw away, the one question this page could not answer.

What it is not: a hit display. The reconstruction stores the fitted lines and the candidates it rejected, not the strip charges they were fitted to, so the sketch on an expanded track is the fit's answer and not the event. Recovering the strips means re-reading the waveforms on EOS, which is a different job. And per RECONSTRUCTION_BASIS.md, geometry here comes from the waveform forward model — never from combined_hits times.
